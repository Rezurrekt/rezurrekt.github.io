    How the island works

    Grass and seasons. Grass regrows fastest in spring and barely at all under snow. Each year, snow arrives and melts on a slightly different date, but hares change coat on a fixed calendar, so in some years their coats are out of step with the snow.

    Hares eat grass and berries, breed in spring and summer, and run when they hear a fox. Longer legs mean faster escapes but burn more food; bigger ears hear foxes sooner; bigger litters mean more, weaker babies. A coat that doesn't match the ground (brown on snow, white on grass) is easier to spot.

    Foxes stalk, then sprint for a few seconds and give up if the hare keeps its lead. They slow down in forest, eat mice and beetles between hares, and breed once a year, with bigger litters when well fed. In cold winters a few may cross the sea ice from the mainland.

    Evolution. Babies inherit the average of both parents' genes plus a small random mutation. Nothing is told to evolve: genes drift toward whatever kept their carriers alive and breeding. Results vary a lot between islands. Try a few, and try long winters.

    What to expect. Hare and fox numbers rise and crash in cycles several years long, with foxes peaking a little after hares. That's the pattern in the Hudson's Bay Company fur records for lynx and snowshoe hares, though the real cycle is closer to ten years. Fox numbers here are kept small so the island stays readable.
